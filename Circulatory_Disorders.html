    Unit 1 of 11 · Infarction

    Infarction: Definition & Mechanisms

    Core idea: Infraction is a tissue death or necrosis due to inadequate blood supply to the affected area.

    1. Definition

    Infraction is a tissue death or necrosis due to inadequate blood supply to the affected area.

    2. Mechanisms of infarction

    Nearly 99% of all infarcts result from thrombotic or embolic events.

    Other mechanisms 4 items

    Other mechanisms include [almost all of them are arterial in origin]:

    1. Local vasospasm
    2. Expansion of atheroma due to hemorrhage into athermotous plaque.
    3. External compression of the vessels. e.g trauma
    4. Entrapment of vessels at hernial sacs.

    Key point. Nearly 99% of all infarcts result from thrombotic or embolic events; the other mechanisms are almost all arterial in origin.

    Quick Review

    • Infarction = tissue death or necrosis due to inadequate blood supply.
    • ~99% thrombotic or embolic.
    • Others (almost all arterial): local vasospasm · hemorrhage into atheromatous plaque expanding the atheroma · external compression (e.g. trauma) · entrapment in hernial sacs.

    Unit 2 of 11 · Infarction

    Factors Determining an Infarct

    Core idea: The development & the size of an infarct are determined by five factors (A–E).

    1. The five factors at a glance

    FactorName
    AThe nature of the vascular supply
    BThe rate of development of occlusion
    CSusceptibility of the tissue for hypoxia
    DOxygen content of the blood
    EThe severity & duration of ischemia

    2. A. The nature of the vascular supply

    A. The nature of the vascular supply: The effect of a dual blood supply (pulumonary artery & bronchial artery for the lung supply - hepatic artery & portal vein for liver supply - radial & ulnar arteries for hand and forearm supply), so if there is an obstruction of one of the arterial supplies, the other one may offset the rapid occurrence of infarction in these organs unlike the renal & splenic circulations which have end arterial supply.

    Dual vs end-arterial supply 5 rows
    Organ / regionSupplyType
    LungPulumonary artery & bronchial arteryDual blood supply
    LiverHepatic artery & portal veinDual blood supply
    Hand and forearmRadial & ulnar arteriesDual blood supply
    Kidney (renal)—End arterial supply
    Spleen (splenic)—End arterial supply

    With a dual supply, if one arterial supply is obstructed, the other one may offset the rapid occurrence of infarction — unlike the renal & splenic circulations, which have end arterial supply.

    3. B. The rate of development of occlusion

    B. The rate of development of occlusion: Slowly developing occlusions are less likely to cause infraction since they provide time for the development of collaterals.

    4. C. Susceptibility of the tissue for hypoxia

    C. Susceptibility of the tissue for hypoxia: The susceptibility of a tissue to hypoxia influences the likelihood of infarction.

    Time to irreversible damage 3 rows
    Cell typeResponse to loss of blood supply
    NeuronsUndergo irreversible damage when deprived of their blood supply for only 3 to 4 minutes.
    Myocardial cellsDie after 20-30 minutes of ischemia.
    FibroblastsMore resistant, especially those in the myocardium.

    5. D. Oxygen content of the blood

    D. Oxygen content of the blood: Partial obstruction of the flow of blood in an anemic or cyanotic patient may lead to tissue infarction.

    6. E. The severity & duration of ischemia

    E. The severity & duration of ischemia.

    Quick Review

    • A — dual supply (lung, liver, hand/forearm) may offset infarction; renal & splenic = end arterial.
    • B — slow occlusion → time for collaterals → less likely to infarct.
    • C — neurons 3–4 min · myocardial cells 20–30 min · fibroblasts more resistant.
    • D — partial obstruction in an anemic or cyanotic patient may cause infarction.
    • E — severity & duration of ischemia.

    Unit 3 of 11 · Infarction

    Types of Infarction: White vs Red

    Core idea: Infarctions are divided by histopathology into two types according the amount of hemorrhaging present.

    1. White vs red infarction

    Comparison 2 types
    FeatureWhite infarctions (anemic infarcts)Red infarctions (hemorrhagic infarcts)
    Organs affectedSolid organs such as the heart, spleen, and kidneysGenerally affecting the lungs
    OcclusionMost often composed of plateletsConsists more of red blood cells and fibrin strands
    AppearanceThe organ becomes white, or pale—

    Key point. White = solid organs (heart, spleen, kidneys), platelet occlusion, pale. Red = lungs, occlusion of red blood cells and fibrin strands.

    Quick Review

    • Classification is by histopathology, according to the amount of hemorrhaging.
    • White (anemic): heart, spleen, kidneys · platelets · white/pale.
    • Red (hemorrhagic): lungs · red blood cells + fibrin strands.

    Unit 4 of 11 · Infarction

    Classification of Infarction by Localization

    Core idea: Infarctions are also classified by localization: heart, brain, lung, spleen, limb, bone, testicle, eye and bowel.

    1. Heart, brain, lung and spleen

    Heart · Brain · Lung · Spleen 4 sites
    SiteDescription in the lecture
    HeartMyocardial infarction (MI), commonly known as a heart attack, is an Infarction of the heart, causing some heart cells to die. The resulting ischemia (restriction in blood supply) and oxygen shortage, if left untreated for a sufficient period of time, can cause damage or kill heart muscle tissue (myocardium).
    BrainCerebral infarction is the ischemic kind of stroke due to a disturbance in the blood vessels supplying blood to the brain. It can be atherothrombotic or embolic.
    LungPulmonary infarction or lung infarction.
    SpleenSplenic infarction occurs when the splenic artery or one of its branches are occluded, for example by a blood clot. Although it can occur asymptomatically, the typical symptom is severe pain in the left upper quadrant of the abdomen, sometimes radiating to the left shoulder. Fever and chills develop in some cases. It has to be differentiated from other causes of acute abdomen.

    2. Limb and bone

    Limb · Bone 2 sites
    SiteDescription in the lecture
    LimbLimb infarction is an infarction of an arm or leg. Causes include arterial embolisms and skeletal muscle infarction as a rare complication of long standing, poorly controlled diabetes mellitus. A major presentation is painful thigh or leg swelling.
    BoneInfarction of bone results in avascular necrosis. Without blood, the bone tissue dies and the bone collapses. If avascular necrosis involves the bones of a joint, it often leads to destruction of the joint articular surfaces.

    3. Testicle, eye and bowel

    Testicle · Eye · Bowel 3 sites
    SiteDescription in the lecture
    TesticleAn infarction of a testicle is commonly caused by testicular torsion and may require removal of the affected testicle.
    EyeAn infarction can occur to the central retinal artery which supplies the retina causing sudden visual loss.
    BowelBowel infarction is generally caused by mesenteric ischemia due to blockages in the arteries or veins that supply the bowel.

    Quick Review

    • Heart → MI (heart attack); untreated ischemia and oxygen shortage can damage or kill the myocardium.
    • Brain → cerebral infarction = ischemic stroke; atherothrombotic or embolic.
    • Spleen → splenic artery/branch occluded; LUQ pain ± radiation to left shoulder; fever and chills in some; differentiate from acute abdomen.
    • Limb → arterial embolisms; skeletal muscle infarction in long-standing poorly controlled DM; painful thigh/leg swelling.
    • Bone → avascular necrosis → bone collapses; joint involvement destroys articular surfaces.
    • Testicle → testicular torsion · Eye → central retinal artery, sudden visual loss · Bowel → mesenteric ischemia (arteries or veins).

    Unit 5 of 11 · Shock

    Shock: Definition & Mechanisms

    Core idea: Shock is failure of the circulatory system to maintain adequate cellular perfusion; it occurs when preload or afterload is decreased, or when the myocardium fails to contract.

    1. Definition

    Shock is a state in which there is failure of the circulatory system to maintain adequate cellular perfusion resulting in widespread reduction in delivery of oxygen & other nutrients to body tissues.

    Shock constitutes systemic hypoperfusion due to reduction either in cardiac out put or in the effective circulating blood volume.

    Values. In shock, the mean arterial pressure is less than 60 mmHg or the systolic blood pressure is less than 90 mmHg.

    1. Hypotension
    2. Impaired tissue perfusion
    3. Cellular hypoxia

    The end results are hypotension followed by impaired tissue perfusion and cellular hypoxia.

    2. What organ perfusion depends on

    Adequate organ perfusion depends on arterial blood pressure (BP) which, in turn, depends on:

    1. Cardiac output (CO)
    2. Peripheral vascular resistance (PVR)

    3. Basic mechanisms → classification

    Therefore, shock (i.e. widespread decreased perfusion of tissues) occurs when the preload (i.e. the blood volume) is decreased, or when the afterload (the peripheral vascular resistance) is decreased, or when the myocardium fails to contract. These basic mechanisms of shock are used to classify it to:

    Basic mechanismClass of shock
    Preload (the blood volume) decreasedA. Hypovolemic shock
    Myocardium fails to contractB. Cardiogenic shock
    Afterload (the peripheral vascular resistance) decreasedC. Distributive shock

    The lecture lists the three mechanisms and the three classes A–C; the details of each class (Unit 6) show which mechanism applies.

    Quick Review

    • Shock = failure to maintain adequate cellular perfusion → widespread reduced delivery of O₂ & nutrients.
    • Systemic hypoperfusion due to ↓ cardiac output or ↓ effective circulating blood volume.
    • MAP < 60 mmHg or systolic BP < 90 mmHg.
    • End results: hypotension → impaired tissue perfusion → cellular hypoxia.
    • BP depends on CO and PVR. Classes: hypovolemic, cardiogenic, distributive.

    Unit 6 of 11 · Shock

    Classification of Shock

    Core idea: Shock is classified into A. Hypovolemic, B. Cardiogenic and C. Distributive shock.

    1. A. Hypovolemic shock

    A. Hypovolemic shock: caused by reduced blood volume. Reduction in circulating blood volume results in the reduction of the preload which leads to inadequate left ventricular filling, reflected as decreased left & right ventricular end diastolic volume and pressure. The reduced preload culminates in decreased cardiac out put which leads to widespread tissue perfusion (shock).

    1. ↓ Circulating blood volume
    2. ↓ Preload
    3. Inadequate LV filling (↓ LV & RV end diastolic volume and pressure)
    4. ↓ Cardiac out put
    5. Shock
    Causes of hypovolumic shock 5 items

    Causes of hypovolumic shock include: Haemorrhage, Diarrhoea & vomiting, Burns, Trauma, and others.

    2. B. Cardiogenic shock

    B. Cardiogenic shock: This is shock that results from severe depression of cardiac performance. It primarily results from pump failure [myocardial failure].

    Causes of cardiogenic shock can be divided into: A. Myopathic and B. Mechanical.

    Myopathic causes 4 items
    1. Acute myocardial infraction. Usually shock occurs in this condition if ≥ 40% of the left ventricular mass & more on the right ventricle is involved by infarction.
    2. Mycocarditis
    3. Dilated cardiomyopathy/hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
    4. Myocardial depression in septic shock
    Mechanical causes 2 groups
    GroupCauses
    IntracardiacSuch as, Left ventricle outflow obstruction, Reduction in forward cardiac output, and others
    ExtracardiacPericardial tamponade (gross fluid accumulation in the pericardial space) results in a decreased ventricular diastolic filling →↓CO.
    Tension pneumothorax (gas accumulation in pleural space)

    3. C. Distributive shock

    C. Distributive shock: refers to a group of shock subtypes caused by profound peripheral vasodilatation despite normal or high cardiac output.

    Causes of distributive shock 4 subtypes
    SubtypeDescription in the lecture
    1. Septic shockA kind of shock caused by systemic microbial infection, it is the commonest among the group & clinically very important.
    2. Neurogenic shockIs a devastating consequence of spinal cord injury that can manifest as hypotension, bradyarrhythmia, and temperature dysregulation. It is associated with cervical and high thoracic spine injury.
    3. Anaphylactic shockInitiated by generalized IgE – mediated hypersensitivity response, associated with systemic vasodilatation & increased vascular permeability.
    4. Endocrine shockThis is a type of shock that typically occurs in adrenal insufficiency.

    Quick Review

    • Hypovolemic: ↓ blood volume → ↓ preload → ↓ CO. Causes: haemorrhage, diarrhoea & vomiting, burns, trauma, others.
    • Cardiogenic: pump (myocardial) failure. Myopathic: acute MI (≥ 40% LV mass), myocarditis, dilated/hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, myocardial depression in septic shock. Mechanical: intracardiac (LV outflow obstruction…) / extracardiac (pericardial tamponade, tension pneumothorax).
    • Distributive: profound peripheral vasodilatation despite normal or high CO — septic (commonest), neurogenic, anaphylactic (IgE), endocrine (adrenal insufficiency).

    Unit 7 of 11 · Shock

    Clinical Course of Shock

    Core idea: Shock presents with a weak and rapid pulse, tachypenia and cool, clammy, cyanotic skin — except early septic shock, where the skin is warm and flushed.

    1. Manifestations

    Patient with shock may manifest as having a weak and rapid pulse, tachypenia, & cool, clammy, cyanotic skin. In septic shock, the skin will initially be warm & flushed because of peripheral vasodilation. The patient may present with confusion, restlessnes, decreased urine output, coma, and death.

    FindingShock (general)Septic shock (initially)
    PulseWeak and rapid—
    BreathingTachypenia—
    SkinCool, clammy, cyanoticWarm & flushed because of peripheral vasodilation
    Other presentationConfusion, restlessnes, decreased urine output, coma, and death

    Quick Review

    • Weak rapid pulse · tachypnoea · cool, clammy, cyanotic skin.
    • Septic shock: skin initially warm & flushed (peripheral vasodilation).
    • Confusion, restlessness, ↓ urine output, coma, death.

    Unit 8 of 11 · IHD

    Ischemic Heart Disease (IHD) & Pathogenesis

    Core idea: IHD is an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand; it includes angina pectoris, myocardial infarction and sudden death.

    1. Definition and forms of IHD

    IHD: an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand results in myocardial hypoxia and accumulation of waste metabolites.

    IHD include the followings:

    1. Angina pectoris
    2. Myocardial infarction
    3. Sudden death

    2. Pathogenesis

    Three mechanisms and their causes 3 groups
    MechanismCauses
    1. Reduction in coronary artery blood flow (90% of the cases)a) Atherosclerosis (the main cause)
    b) Coronary artery spasm
    c) Hemodynamic disorders (circulatory disorders)
    d) Non atherosclerotic coronary diseases (arteritis)
    2. Increase cardiac demanda) Tachycardia
    b) Ventricular hypertrophy
    3. Reduced oxygen carrying capacity of the blooda) Anemia
    b) Cigarette smoking
    c) Advanced lung diseases
    d) Cyanotic congenital heart diseases
    e) Carbon monoxide poisoning

    Key point. Reduction in coronary artery blood flow accounts for 90% of the cases, and atherosclerosis is the main cause.

    Quick Review

    • IHD = O₂ supply/demand imbalance → myocardial hypoxia + accumulation of waste metabolites.
    • Forms: angina pectoris · MI · sudden death.
    • ↓ coronary flow (90%): atherosclerosis (main), spasm, hemodynamic disorders, arteritis.
    • ↑ demand: tachycardia, ventricular hypertrophy.
    • ↓ O₂-carrying capacity: anemia, smoking, advanced lung disease, cyanotic congenital heart disease, CO poisoning.

    Unit 9 of 11 · IHD

    Angina Pectoris

    Core idea: Angina pectoris is recurrent retrosternal chest discomfort or pain caused by transient myocardial ischemia that is not sufficient to induce infarction.

    1. Definition

    Angina pectoris: recurrent retrosternal chest discomfort or pain caused by transient myocardial ischemia. This ischemia is not sufficient to induce infarction. There are three overlapping pattern of angina:

    2. Three overlapping patterns

    Stable · Prinzmetal (variant) · Unstable 3 patterns
    PatternDescription in the lecture
    1. Stable anginaChronic transient angina pectoris, precipitated by physical activity or emotional upset, relieved by rest within a few minutes. Usually occurs when about 75% of coronary artery lumen stenosed by atheroma (chronic cause).
    2. Prinzmetal (variant) anginaTypical anginal discomfort, usually at rest, which develops because of coronary artery spasm, rather than an increase of myocardial oxygen demand.
    3. Unstable anginaAcute coronary syndrome characterized by progressively increased frequency and more prolonged attack of angina. It is usually occur due to disrupted atherosclerotic plaque with superimposed thrombosis. It has high frequency of progression to myocardial infarction if untreated.

    3. Types of angina — figure

    Figure labels 4 vessels · 3 types
    Type (figure)Vessel shownMechanism labelIschemia label
    NormalNormal coronary artery——
    Stable angina (chronic stable angina)AtherosclerosisFixed stenosisDemand ischemia
    Unstable anginaAtherosclerosis with blood clotThrombusSupply ischemia
    Variant angina (Printzmetal's variant angina)Coronary spasmVasospasmSupply ischemia

    Quick Review

    • Stable: exertion/emotion, relieved by rest within minutes; ~75% lumen stenosis by atheroma; fixed stenosis → demand ischemia.
    • Prinzmetal (variant): at rest; coronary artery spasm, not ↑ O₂ demand → supply ischemia.
    • Unstable: acute coronary syndrome; ↑ frequency, more prolonged; disrupted plaque + superimposed thrombosis; high progression to MI if untreated.

    Unit 10 of 11 · IHD

    Myocardial Infarction (MI)

    Core idea: MI is a region of myocardial necrosis, usually due to complete cessation of blood supply; it is transmural (≈90%) or subendothelial.

    1. Definition

    MI: Region of myocardial necrosis usually due to complete cessation of blood supply; most often results from acute thrombus at site of coronary atherosclerotic stenosis. It may be the first clinical manifestation of ischemic heart disease, or there may be a history of angina pectoris.

    2. Types of MI

    Transmural vs subendothelial 2 types
    TypeDescription in the lecture
    1. Transmural MI(Full wall thikness) which is most common type about 90% of the cases. There is complete occlusion to the blood supply of the area by thrombus formation in ruptured already present atheroma.
    2. Subendothelial MIThe subendothelium is the most vulnerable region to any reduction in blood flow. In this type there is advanced but not sever coronary atherosclerosis lead to subendothelial infarction only.

    3. Morphological features of MI

    Macroscopical · Microscopical 2 views
    ViewFeatures
    A. MacroscopicalPale infarcted area including the left ventricle and interventricular septum. In subendothelial type the infarction is limited to the inner third of left ventricles. Infarcted heart less than 12 hour not suitable for gross examination.
    B. MicroscopicalThe myocardial cells show coagulative necrosis and this is not be detectable for the first 4 to 8 hours. The infarcted area infilterated by acute inflammatory cells and if the patient survive the infarcted area replaced by granulation tissue and healed by fibrosis.
    1. Coagulative necrosis (not detectable for the first 4–8 h)
    2. Acute inflammatory cells
    3. Granulation tissue (if the patient survive)
    4. Fibrosis

    Quick Review

    • MI: myocardial necrosis, usually complete cessation of blood supply; most often acute thrombus at a coronary atherosclerotic stenosis.
    • May be the first manifestation of IHD, or follow a history of angina.
    • Transmural (full thickness, ~90%) — thrombus in a ruptured pre-existing atheroma.
    • Subendothelial — advanced but not severe atherosclerosis; limited to the inner third of the LV.
    • Gross: pale area in LV + septum; < 12 h not suitable for gross exam. Micro: coagulative necrosis (not seen in first 4–8 h) → acute inflammation → granulation tissue → fibrosis.

    Unit 11 of 11 · IHD

    Sudden Death

    Core idea: Sudden death is unexpected death from cardiac causes early after symptom onset or without symptoms; it is usually the first clinical manifestation of IHD.

    1. Definition

    It is unexpected death from cardiac causes early after symptom onset (usually within hour) or without onset of symptoms (a symptomatic). Usually it is the first clinical manifestation of IHD.

    2. Causes in young patients

    In young patient it could be due to:

    1. Congenital coronary abnormalities
    2. Aortic artery stenosis
    3. Mitral valve prolapse
    4. Myocarditis and cardiomyopathy
    5. Arrhythmia

    Quick Review

    • Unexpected cardiac death, usually within an hour of symptom onset, or without symptoms.
    • Usually the first clinical manifestation of IHD.
    • Young patients: congenital coronary abnormalities, aortic stenosis, mitral valve prolapse, myocarditis & cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia.
